How to use this book

Most businesses don't fail for lack of a good product. They fail because the operations underneath can't hold the weight of growth. This framework gives you that structure, one layer at a time.

The five layers (Pancha Khosha)

In Indian philosophy, the Pancha Kosha are the five sheaths of a human being, from the physical body to the innermost layer of fulfilment. At Khosha Systems we use the same idea for companies. A business is healthy only when every layer is healthy, and each layer depends on the one beneath it.

1 · AnnamayaFoundation. Legal entity, registrations, compliance, books and cash.
2 · PranamayaPeople. Hiring, onboarding, attendance, payroll and performance.
3 · ManomayaSystems. SOPs, products and services catalogue, pricing and the digital stack.
4 · VijnanamayaGrowth. Strategy, KPIs, sales pipeline, marketing and the operating rhythm.
5 · AnandamayaScale. Raising investment, franchising and building a business that lasts.

Three ways to use it

  1. As a diagnostic. Take the 25-point Business Health Scorecard on the next pages. Your lowest-scoring layer is where you start.
  2. As a checklist binder. Print the chapters you need. Every checklist uses tick boxes (☐) and every template has blank rows you can fill in by hand or copy into a spreadsheet.
  3. As a digital system. The templates/ folder that ships with this book has the same templates as an Excel workbook, CSV files and editable Markdown documents. Load them into Google Sheets, Notion, Zoho or your own software.

Important note

This book is general guidance, not legal, tax or investment advice. Indian rules on GST, labour codes, the Startup India scheme and company law change often. Figures here were checked in November 2026. Confirm deadlines, thresholds and contract terms with your Chartered Accountant, Company Secretary or lawyer before acting. Outside India, use the checklists as a structure and swap in your local rules.

1.2 Registration and licence checklist (India)

  • Entity incorporation (MCA SPICe+ for companies, FiLLiP for LLPs)
  • PAN and TAN for the entity
  • Current bank account in the business name
  • GST registration: mandatory once turnover crosses ₹40 lakh (goods) or ₹20 lakh (services), with lower limits in special-category states. Required from day one for inter-state supply of goods and most e-commerce selling
  • Shops & Establishment registration (state law, usually within 30 days of opening)
  • Professional Tax registration (in states that levy it, such as Karnataka and Maharashtra)
  • Udyam (MSME) registration: free, and unlocks priority lending and delayed-payment protection
  • EPF registration once you have 20 or more employees
  • ESI registration once you have 10 or more employees (wage ceiling applies)
  • Trademark application for brand name and logo (Class-wise, via IP India)
  • Industry licences: FSSAI (food), Drug licence, Trade licence, IEC (import/export), RERA (real estate), and so on
  • DPIIT Startup India recognition, if you qualify (see Chapter 05)
  • Domain name, business email and a basic website
  • Founders' agreement (vesting, roles, IP assignment, exit terms)

1.4 The bookkeeping routine

Daily (10 minutes)

  • Raise invoices for everything delivered today
  • Record cash sales and petty cash
  • Upload bills and receipts (photograph them)
  • Check the bank balance against expected receipts

Weekly (30 minutes)

  • Categorise all bank transactions
  • Send payment reminders for overdue invoices
  • Schedule vendor payments
  • Update the 13-week cash-flow forecast

Monthly (half a day)

  • Reconcile every bank and card account
  • Run payroll and deduct TDS, PF, ESI and PT
  • Pay statutory dues and file GST returns
  • Review the P&L, balance sheet and receivables ageing
  • Compare actuals to budget

Annually

  • Physical inventory and fixed-asset count
  • Statutory audit (companies) and tax audit (if applicable)
  • Income-tax return, ROC annual filings
  • Set next year's budget and pricing

Chapter 02 · Pranamaya · People

Hiring, onboarding and attendance

The life force of your business. Good systems let ordinary people do extraordinary work, and good onboarding decides whether a hire stays three years or three weeks.

2.1 The 8-step hiring process

  1. Define the role. Write the outcomes the role owns in its first 12 months, not just a task list. Set the salary band before you advertise.
  2. Write the job description: purpose, 5–7 key responsibilities, must-have and nice-to-have skills, location, salary range, and how to apply.
  3. Source through referrals first (they're the most reliable), then job portals, LinkedIn, campus and local networks.
  4. Screen with a 15-minute phone call on motivation, salary expectations, notice period and deal-breakers.
  5. Assess with a paid or short practical task that mirrors the real job.
  6. Interview with a structured scorecard (same questions, scored 1–5) and at least two interviewers.
  7. Check references (two minimum) and verify documents and background.
  8. Offer in writing, then issue the appointment letter on joining.

2.2 Appointment letter checklist

India's four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, replacing 29 older central labour laws. One visible change: the OSH Code requires employers to give every employee an appointment letter, including fixed-term and contract staff. Make sure each letter covers:

  • Employee name, designation and employee ID
  • Date of joining and place of work
  • Category: permanent, probation, fixed-term or contract
  • Wages: basic pay, allowances, CTC breakdown
  • Wage structure checked against the Codes' 50% "wages" rule for PF and gratuity
  • Working hours, weekly off, overtime terms
  • Leave entitlements
  • Probation period and confirmation process
  • Notice period and termination terms
  • Social security: PF, ESI, gratuity, insurance
  • Confidentiality, IP assignment and code of conduct
  • Reporting manager and grievance mechanism

State rules under the Codes were still being notified through 2026. Have an employment lawyer check your template.

Chapter 03 · Manomaya · Systems

SOPs, products and services

The mind of your business: how work gets done, what you sell, and the software that holds it together. Systems are what let you step back from day-to-day work, and later what you sell to investors and franchisees.

3.1 How to write an SOP in 7 steps

  1. List every recurring task. Rank them by frequency × cost of a mistake. Document the top 10 first.
  2. Record the best person doing the task: a screen recording or phone video with narration.
  3. Turn it into numbered steps, each starting with a verb ("Open…", "Check…", "Send…").
  4. Add screenshots, the trigger (when it starts), the owner, and what "done" looks like.
  5. Test it: someone new follows the SOP alone. Fix wherever they get stuck.
  6. Store all SOPs in one searchable place (Notion, Google Drive or your intranet).
  7. Review every SOP every 6 months, or whenever the process or tool changes.

Chapter 05 · Anandamaya · Scale I

How to get investors in your company

Outside capital works like fuel: useful only when the engine (Layers 1–4) is sound. This chapter explains your funding options, how to prepare, and how a round actually runs.

5.1 Should you raise at all?

Raise equity when…

  • The market is large and timing matters (winner-takes-most)
  • You've shown customers want it (paying users, repeat orders, growth)
  • Each rupee invested can return several rupees of enterprise value
  • You're ready to give up a share of ownership and report to a board

Consider alternatives when…

  • The business is profitable and can grow from its own cash
  • You need working capital, not growth capital (use debt)
  • The market is steady and local (franchising or loans may fit better)
  • You want full control and dividends rather than an exit

5.3 Investor-readiness checklist

  • Incorporated as a Private Limited Company
  • DPIIT Startup India recognition (eligible companies and LLPs up to 10 years old, below the turnover cap; check the 2026 notification for current limits)
  • Founders' agreement with vesting (typically 4 years, 1-year cliff)
  • All IP (code, brand, designs) assigned to the company, not the founders
  • Trademark filed
  • Clean, up-to-date books and audited financials for past years
  • All ROC, GST, TDS and PF filings current, with no outstanding notices
  • Cap table in a spreadsheet that ties back to MCA records
  • Employee contracts, appointment letters and an ESOP pool plan
  • Key customer contracts in writing
  • Financial model: 3 years monthly, with assumptions
  • Pitch deck (12 slides) plus a one-page teaser
  • KPI dashboard with 12+ months of history
  • Use of funds and an 18–24-month milestone plan
  • Data room organised (see 5.6)
  • A list of 50–100 target investors who fit your stage and sector
On angel tax: the old Section 56(2)(viib) "angel tax" on share premiums was abolished by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024. You still need a proper valuation report and FEMA compliance for foreign investors, and DPIIT recognition still unlocks benefits such as the Section 80-IAC tax holiday (on application), IPR fee rebates, self-certification under some labour laws and Seed Fund access.

5.4 The 12-step fundraising process

  1. Decide the amount and the milestones. Raise enough for 18–24 months of runway to reach the milestones that justify the next round.
  2. Choose the instrument: equity shares, CCPS (compulsorily convertible preference shares, the Indian VC standard), CCDs, convertible notes (allowed for DPIIT startups), or iSAFE notes for quick early rounds.
  3. Build the financial model and valuation logic. Early valuations are negotiated. Dilution of 10–25% per round is typical.
  4. Prepare the deck, teaser and data room before contacting anyone.
  5. Build the investor list by stage, sector, cheque size and portfolio conflicts. Rank them A/B/C.
  6. Get warm introductions through portfolio founders, mentors, CAs and lawyers. Cold emails work best with a crisp teaser and strong traction.
  7. Run a tight process. Pitch your B-list first for practice, then pack the A-list meetings into 4–6 weeks so you create momentum.
  8. Follow up with monthly investor updates. They show progress and build trust even with investors who say "not now".
  9. Negotiate the term sheet. Focus on valuation, liquidation preference, board, anti-dilution, ESOP pool and vesting (see 5.7).
  10. Due diligence: financial, legal, tax and technical. A clean data room cuts this from months to weeks.
  11. Definitive agreements: Share Subscription Agreement (SSA) and Shareholders' Agreement (SHA). Amend the Articles of Association.
  12. Close and comply: receive funds, allot shares (PAS-3 to the ROC), FEMA filings (FC-GPR) for foreign money, update the cap table and statutory registers. Then deliver on the use-of-funds plan.

Example: the investor puts in ₹2 crore for 2,00,000 new shares at ₹100 each, and the ESOP pool is topped up by 50,000 shares in the same round. That makes the post-money valuation ₹12.5 crore (12,50,000 shares × ₹100) and the pre-money ₹10.5 crore. Because the pool top-up counts in the price, it dilutes the founders rather than the investor: a common negotiation point. The workbook version calculates this automatically.

Seven fundraising mistakes to avoid

  1. Raising with messy books and late filings
  2. Giving away too much equity too early (keep founders above 50–60% after seed)
  3. Taking money from someone who doesn't fit (wrong stage, sector or values)
  4. Fundraising for too long. Set a deadline and keep running the business
  5. Signing a term sheet without a startup lawyer
  6. Mixing investment money with operating money without a budget
  7. Going silent with investors after the money lands
Chapter 06 · Anandamaya · Scale II

Steps to start your own franchise

Franchising lets you grow with other people's capital and local energy, but only if your business is a system someone else can copy and run profitably. Read this chapter as the franchisor: the brand owner.

6.1 Franchise readiness test

Answer yes or no. You need at least 8 yes answers before you franchise.

  • At least one unit (ideally 2–3) has been profitable for 2+ years
  • Results are repeatable in a different location or city
  • The business runs to documented SOPs without the founder
  • Unit economics leave enough margin for the franchisee AND your royalty
  • The brand has recognition and customer loyalty
  • Trademarks are registered in the relevant classes
  • The model can be taught in 2–6 weeks of training
  • The supply chain can serve outlets in new geographies
  • You have the capital and team to support franchisees (launch, audits, marketing)
  • Market demand exists in your target cities

6.2 Choose a franchise model

ModelWho owns and investsWho operatesBest when

COCO (company-owned, company-operated)BrandBrandFlagship and pilot stores. Not a franchise, but proves the model

6.3 The 12 steps to franchise your business

  1. Prove the prototype. Make one or more units consistently profitable, then document their exact P&L.
  2. Protect the brand. Register trademarks (name, logo, tagline) in every relevant class before you sign anyone. Record recipes, designs and software as trade secrets or copyright.
  3. Design unit economics for the franchisee. Total investment, payback period (aim for 18–36 months), breakeven month, and expected ROI after royalty.
  4. Set your fee structure (see 6.4) and choose the model (FOFO, FOCO and so on).
  5. Write the operations manual (see 6.6). This is the heart of the franchise.
  6. Draft the legal documents with a franchise lawyer: Franchise Agreement, Franchise Disclosure Document, NDA, Letter of Intent, and a trademark licence (if separate).
  7. Build the support system: training academy, launch kit, supply chain, POS/ERP, marketing playbook, field audit team.
  8. Define the ideal franchisee and a scoring process (see 6.8). Choose operators, not just investors.
  9. Create sales material: franchise brochure, website page, investment table, testimonials, unit P&L.
  10. Recruit: franchise expos, franchise portals, LinkedIn, existing customers (often the best franchisees), brokers.
  11. Launch the first 3–5 franchisees near home so you can support them closely. Fix the system before you expand further.
  12. Scale with governance: monthly reviews, mystery audits, a franchise advisory council, a shared marketing fund, and technology that gives you live visibility of every outlet.

6.7 Franchise agreement and disclosure checklist

India has no single franchise statute. Franchising is governed by contract law, trademark law, competition law, GST and FEMA (for cross-border deals). That makes your agreement and a voluntary disclosure document doubly important. In the United States, by contrast, the FTC Franchise Rule requires franchisors to give a Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) with 23 specified items at least 14 calendar days before signing or payment, and several states also require registration. If you franchise abroad, use a local franchise lawyer.

Franchise agreement clauses

  • Grant of rights and trademark licence
  • Territory and exclusivity
  • Term, renewal and conditions
  • All fees, royalty basis, payment dates, GST
  • Site approval and fit-out standards
  • Training and ongoing support obligations
  • Approved suppliers and pricing
  • Reporting, audit and inspection rights
  • Marketing fund rules
  • Confidentiality and IP protection
  • Non-compete (during the term) and non-solicit
  • Transfer, sale and succession
  • Default, cure period and termination
  • Post-termination obligations (de-branding)
  • Dispute resolution: arbitration seat and governing law

Disclosure document contents

  • Franchisor background, directors and experience
  • Litigation and bankruptcy history
  • Initial and ongoing fees in full
  • Estimated total initial investment
  • Supplier restrictions and franchisor margins
  • Territory rules
  • Training and support provided
  • Financial performance data (if shared), with assumptions
  • Number of outlets opened, closed and transferred
  • Contacts of current and former franchisees
  • Franchisor's financial statements
  • A copy of the franchise agreement
